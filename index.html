This map shows documented Indigenous and fur-trade routes around Dauphin Lake, Manitoba.       What this map does and doesn't show

      Shaded rings mark convergence points: portages, the river mouth and the lake shore with its succession of posts. The written record shows that traffic had to pass through these places. They are not measurements of how many people travelled.

      Camp sites are not plotted. Manitoba's Historic Resources Branch does not publish the locations of archaeological sites, and many seasonal camp and harvesting places are knowledge held by First Nations. For those, contact the Fort Dauphin Museum's Parkland Archaeological Laboratory, the Historic Resources Branch, and the West Region Tribal Council and its member Nations.

      Not drawn: the tributaries that feed Dauphin Lake from the west and south, which were probable routes inland toward Riding and Duck Mountains: the Valley, Vermilion, Wilson, Ochre and Turtle rivers and Mink River. The base map isn't detailed enough to draw them reliably.

      Archaeology background: the Fort Dauphin Museum's Parkland Archaeological Laboratory holds information on more than 1,000 sites in the region.       About this view

      Ridges are cells that stand higher than their surroundings within the search radius (topographic position index). In this region the long, narrow rises below the Riding Mountain escarpment are mostly old beaches of glacial Lake Agassiz. Hind's 1858 map shows cart trails on them, and the 1938 park guide calls them the old travel highways. Small radius and threshold pick out low beach ridges; large values pick out the escarpment and hills.

      Resolution: the tiles give roughly 75 m cells for focus areas and about 300 m for the overview. Beach ridges a few metres high may be smoothed out. For detail, use the travelscape framework with Manitoba LiDAR or HRDEM.

      Ridges were travel routes and are well documented as such. They are not a guide to where to search. A heritage permit is needed to search for or collect artifacts in Manitoba, and nothing may be removed from Riding Mountain National Park.

      About this network

      Every connection comes from a dated entry in Peter Fidler's Fort Dauphin journals (HBCA B.51/a/2–3) or the district reports, as transcribed in G. J. Ens, Peter Fidler and the Fur Trade of the Red River and Manitobah Districts 1812–1823 (2024). Each one cites a page, and every short quote was checked against that page.

      Types: journey means people travelled the leg; supply means goods or provisions were moved; correspondence means letters or papers were carried; trade means trips to Indigenous camps; administrative means one post was an outpost or sub-post of another.

      Positions: some posts come from the MHS and Parks Canada. Others are georeferenced from the book's Map 2-3 (±10–18 km) or estimated from Fidler's distances. Indigenous camps are shown only as general areas with 20–30 km rings, at the scale Fidler gives.

      Scope: this is a first pass of 39 connections, not every entry in the journals. A full day-by-day extraction would add weather, loads, travel times and many more trips.

      About 13,000 to 9,000 years ago, glacial Lake Agassiz covered most of Manitoba. As it drained in stages, each pause left a beach: a low ridge of sand and gravel, usually 1–5 m high and 50–300 m wide. Many now run as pale, curving bands across the fields below the Manitoba Escarpment. This tab estimates where each named shoreline should run from its measured height and the tilt of the land since the ice left. It then lets you open any spot in Google Earth or Google Maps satellite view to look for the ridge yourself.

      How the shorelines are drawn

      The land has risen unevenly since the ice melted: more in the northeast, less in the southwest. So each old water surface is now a tilted plane, not a level line. This tab treats each shoreline as a plane rising about 0.25 m per km toward N30°E. That is Johnston's (1946) slope measured at Arden, set in the northeast direction reported for later shorelines of Dauphin Lake and Lake Winnipegosis. The Upper Campbell plane is anchored at 333 m at Arden and checks out against Cowan (367 m) and Morden (320 m) to within about 5 m. Each other beach is anchored where it was measured.

      Expect errors of about ±10 m, and more far from each anchor. Older beaches are tilted more steeply and younger ones less so; the tilt slider lets you test this. The elevation tiles are about 30–75 m cells, which can smooth a 2 m ridge away. A coloured band shows where to look, not where a ridge has been mapped. The Manitoba Geological Survey's surficial maps (GeoFile 1-2022, Matile & Keller 2017) show the mapped ridges.

      Finding a ridge in satellite images

      • Pale curving bands in bare or stubble fields: sandy, gravelly soil dries out and looks lighter than the dark clay or till around it.
      • Crop stress strips in dry summers: the coarse soil holds little water, so crops on the ridge yellow first.
      • Strings of gravel pits along one line. Near Ste. Rose, the north end of the Ste. Amelie ridge has been dug into almost one long pit.
      • Roads, shelterbelts, farmyards and cemeteries that bend to follow a gentle rise instead of the survey grid.
      • Best seasons: spring after snowmelt, late fall after harvest, or a dry spell. In Google Earth Pro, the historical imagery slider lets you compare years.

      Look-alikes: eskers (winding, cross the slope), moraines (hummocky), sand dunes (blotchy, often on the beaches themselves), drainage ditches and old road grades (dead straight), and ridges left by uneven settling of the ground.

      The ridges as travel routes

      The beaches were dry, raised and well drained in country that was otherwise wet. The Manitoba Historical Society records that the Arden Ridge carried a branch of the Fort Ellice (Carlton) Trail, known as the Old Dauphin Trail and the Beautiful Plains Ridge. An older Indigenous trail ran from Sandy Bay across the ridge toward Riding Mountain. Settlers later followed the same line, and the 1891 Burrows colonization road ran along it. Provincial Road 352 is built on the barrier beach today.

      The pattern repeats elsewhere. Highway 10 follows the Campbell beach along the foot of the escarpment and runs on a beach ridge north of Dauphin. Highway 50 follows the Burnside beach. Farmsteads, churches, cemeteries and gravel pits cluster on the ridges because they are the driest, firmest ground.

      Found something that might be old: a stone tool, pottery, bone, a trade bead, a hearth showing in a cutbank? This page helps you record it properly and send the report to the people who look after Manitoba's heritage. Please read the first box before you do anything else.

      First: leave it where it is

      1. Don't dig, pull or collect. Where an object lies tells archaeologists most of what it means. Under The Heritage Resources Act, artifacts belong to the Province. Searching for or collecting them needs a heritage permit, and so does metal detecting at known or potential sites.
      2. If it could be human bone, stop. Leave it, keep others away, and call the RCMP and the Historic Resources Branch, (204) 945-2118.
      3. Take photos where it lies. Put a coin, pen or ruler beside it for scale. Take one close-up and one wide shot showing the surroundings.
      4. Record where you are with the location tools below, while you're standing at the spot.
      5. On private land or a reserve, tell the landowner or the band office. In Riding Mountain National Park, tell Parks Canada. Nothing may be removed from the park.
